A Capesize isn't just a big Supramax. Bigger holds are taller, which means more upper structure that's hard to reach, more surface area, more water and more time. If you plan a Capesize job the way you'd plan a Handysize, you'll run out of people, water and patience.

Here's how hold cleaning scales with ship size, and what to plan for each.

## Bulk carrier sizes at a glance

| Type | Typical deadweight | Typical holds | Notes for cleaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Handysize | ~15,000–40,000 dwt | 5 | Smaller holds; usually geared; crew can often manage routine changes |
| Supramax / Ultramax | ~50,000–65,000 dwt | 5 | The workhorse of minor bulk trades; frequent cargo changes, including dirty-to-grain |
| Panamax / Kamsarmax | ~65,000–85,000 dwt | 7 | More holds; usually gearless; grain, coal and ore trades |
| Capesize | ~100,000–200,000+ dwt | 9 | Mostly ore and coal; very tall holds; grain cleaning less common but a big job when needed |

Figures are typical; individual designs vary.

## What changes with size

**Number of holds.** Five, seven or nine holds is a big difference in total work. More holds also means more opportunity to work in parallel, if you have the people.

**Hold height.** Taller holds mean more upper structure out of reach from the tank top. On a Supramax, the upper frames are already well beyond the reach of ground-based tools; on a Capesize, they're far higher still. Access to the upper structure becomes the dominant planning issue.

**Surface area.** Total internal surface area grows quickly with size. More area means more chemical, more water and more hours.

**Cargo mix.** Handysize and Supramax ships change cargoes frequently, often between dirty and clean cargoes, so they clean to grain more often. Capesizes mostly carry ore and coal, so grain cleaning is rarer, but when it happens it's a big job.

## Handysize

- 5 holds, smaller and lower
- Crew can often handle routine changes
- Dirty-to-grain changes still benefit from extra hands, especially for the upper structure
- Typical shore gang: a small team working one or two holds at a time

## Supramax / Ultramax

- 5 holds, taller than a Handysize
- Frequent cargo changes; dirty-to-grain is common
- Upper structure is a real access challenge; rope access is often the fastest solution
- Typical team: 6–12 for a shore gang; riding crews of 4–12 depending on passage length
- Pumps: two HP units plus a spare is a common minimum

## Panamax / Kamsarmax

- 7 holds, so 40% more holds than a Supramax
- Often in grain trades, so grain clean is common
- Plan more people and equipment to work several holds at once
- Pumps: at least two to three HP units plus spares, and one or two combi-gun lines
- Water: plan fresh water for seven holds, not five

## Capesize

- 9 holds, very tall
- Mostly ore and coal; normal or shovel clean is the norm
- When a higher standard is needed, the upper structure is the dominant problem. Plan rope access or other access early
- Large teams and multiple HP units
- Large fresh water requirement

## How many men are required to clean 5 holds?

For a five-hold Handysize or Supramax:

- **Routine change (normal clean):** the ship's crew, or a small team, can usually manage
- **Dirty-to-grain:** 6–12 people working several holds in parallel, plus rope access for the upper structure if needed

See [how long hold cleaning takes](/blog/how-long-does-hold-cleaning-take) for more on timings and manpower.

## How many pumps are needed?

A practical rule is one HP unit per team working in a hold, plus a spare. For a seven-hold Panamax working two or three holds at once, that's three or four units. For combi-gun washing, the ship's fire pump or a dedicated hold-washing pump. More in our [equipment guide](/blog/hold-cleaning-equipment).

## How much water?

Water requirements scale with surface area. As a rough guide:

- An HP unit at 30 l/min uses about 1.8 tonnes of water per hour
- Combi-gun washing uses far more, often tens of tonnes per hour
- The fresh water rinse is the critical part. Plan it in tonnes per hold, multiplied by the number of holds, with a margin

Larger ships need proportionally more fresh water. Make sure it's on board before cleaning starts. See [high-pressure washing](/blog/high-pressure-washing-cargo-holds) for more on water consumption.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many cargo holds does a bulk carrier have?
Typically five on a Handysize or Supramax, seven on a Panamax or Kamsarmax, and nine on a Capesize, though designs vary.

### How many men are required to clean 5 holds?
For a routine change, the ship's crew or a small team. For a dirty-to-grain change on a five-hold ship, 6 to 12 people working several holds in parallel, plus rope access for the upper structure if needed.

### How many HP pumps are needed for a Panamax?
At least two to three HP units plus spares for a seven-hold Panamax working two or three holds at a time, along with one or two combi-gun lines.

### Is Capesize hold cleaning different from Supramax?
Yes. Capesize holds are much taller and more numerous, so access to the upper structure, surface area, water and team size all become bigger planning issues.

### Why do Supramax ships clean to grain so often?
Supramax and Ultramax ships trade minor bulks and change cargoes frequently, often between dirty cargoes like coal or petcoke and clean cargoes like grain.
