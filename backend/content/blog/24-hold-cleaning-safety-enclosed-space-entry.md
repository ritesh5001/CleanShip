A cargo hold doesn't look like an enclosed space. It's huge, it's open to the sky when the hatches are up, and people walk in and out of it every day in port. That's exactly why it catches people out. Holds have killed seafarers through oxygen depletion and toxic gas, often when someone went in without testing because it "looked fine."

Hold cleaning adds its own hazards on top: high-pressure water, chemicals, work at height, and long hours in heat. None of it needs to be dangerous, but all of it needs managing.

## Is a cargo hold an enclosed space?

Yes. IMO guidance describes an enclosed space as one with limited openings for entry and exit, inadequate ventilation and not designed for continuous worker occupancy, and cargo holds have long been among the examples it gives. They fit the description, particularly with the hatches closed. Treat them accordingly: test, ventilate, permit, monitor.

## What gases can be present in a cargo hold?

It depends on the previous cargo and what's happened since:

| Hazard | Common causes |
|---|---|
| Oxygen deficiency | Rusting steel consuming oxygen in a closed hold; wood products, some grains, coal and metal concentrates absorbing oxygen; inert gas or fumigation |
| Carbon monoxide | Self-heating coal; wood pellets; smouldering residue |
| Methane (flammable) | Coal |
| Hydrogen sulphide | Some sulphur cargoes; rotting organic residue |
| Ammonia | Some fertilisers (urea, ammonium compounds) |
| Phosphine | Residual fumigant from grain or other agricultural cargoes |
| Hydrocarbon vapour | Green petcoke |
| Chemical vapour | Cleaning chemicals in a poorly ventilated hold |

## Is oxygen deficiency possible?

Yes, and it's the hazard that kills most often. Oxygen can be consumed by rusting steel, by cargoes that absorb oxygen, or displaced by other gases. You can't see it, smell it or feel it coming. Below normal oxygen levels, judgement and coordination go first, so the person affected often doesn't realise anything is wrong. Rescuers who rush in without breathing apparatus often become the next casualties.

## How should a cargo hold be gas tested?

- Test **before** entry, from outside the hold, using a calibrated multi-gas detector
- Test at **several levels**: top, middle and bottom. Some gases are heavier than air and pool low, others rise
- Test for **oxygen, flammable gas, carbon monoxide and hydrogen sulphide** as a minimum, plus anything specific to the cargo (for example phosphine after fumigated cargoes, ammonia after some fertilisers)
- Keep **monitoring** while people are inside, with personal gas detectors as required by the ship's procedure
- Retest after any **break in work** or change in conditions

Long-standing IMO guidance set entry criteria of a steady 21% oxygen by volume, flammable gas below 1% of the lower flammable limit and toxic gases below 50% of the occupational exposure limit. The IMO revised its enclosed space recommendations again in 2025 (resolution MSC.581(110)), so make sure your company procedure reflects the current version, and follow it, since it may be stricter.

## How should ventilation be arranged?

- Open hatches where safe and practicable: holds with open hatches ventilate far better
- Use mechanical ventilation where fitted, or portable fans
- Arrange ventilation to reach the lowest parts of the hold, where heavy gases collect
- Ventilate before entry and continue during work
- Never use pure oxygen to "freshen" a space. It creates a severe fire risk

## Can crew enter a hold immediately after washing?

Not without testing. Washing itself can change the atmosphere: chemicals give off vapour, warm water raises humidity, and closing hatches during washing reduces ventilation. Test before re-entry after any break.

## What permits are required for hold entry?

Follow the ship's safety management system. Typically:

- **Enclosed space entry permit** with gas test results, ventilation arrangements, communication and rescue plan
- **Permit to work** for the cleaning job
- **Working at height permit** for work on the upper structure
- **Hot work permit** if any hot work is involved

The permit should be issued by a responsible officer, displayed at the entry point, and closed out when the work is done.

## Can crew work alone inside a hold?

No. Enclosed space entry procedures require a competent attendant at the entrance, in communication with those inside, and nobody should work alone in a hold. The attendant should not enter in an emergency without proper equipment and backup. Their job is to raise the alarm.

## The risks of high-pressure water

High-pressure water injection injuries are serious. A jet at 500 bar can penetrate skin and inject water and contaminants deep into tissue. The wound may look like a small cut, but it needs urgent hospital treatment. Controls:

- Trained operators only
- Never point the lance at anyone, including your own feet
- Face shield, waterproof suit, safety boots, gloves
- Dead-man trigger, never tied open
- Check hoses and fittings before use
- Depressurise before disconnecting
- Keep others clear of the spray zone
- Report any injection injury immediately

## The risks of chemical cleaning

- Burns to skin and eyes from alkalis and acids
- Inhalation of spray mist and vapour
- Reactions if incompatible chemicals are mixed
- Slippery surfaces

Controls: follow the safety data sheet, wear chemical-resistant PPE, ventilate, never mix products, keep an eyewash station at hand. See our [hold cleaning chemicals guide](/blog/hold-cleaning-chemicals) for more.

## What PPE is required?

At minimum:

- Hard hat, safety boots, gloves
- Waterproof clothing
- Eye and face protection (face shield for HP work)
- Hearing protection
- Respiratory protection for dust and chemical mist
- Chemical-resistant gloves and suit for chemical work
- Harness and fall protection for work at height
- Personal gas detector as required

## Working at height

Upper structure cleaning means working 10–20 metres above the tank top. Use proper access equipment, harnesses and fall arrest, and keep work at height out of heavy weather at sea. Rope access must be done by certified technicians with a rescue plan. See [rope access hold cleaning](/blog/cleaning-cargo-hold-upper-frames-without-scaffolding).

## Other everyday hazards

- Slips on wet, chemical-covered or residue-covered surfaces
- Heat stress in hot holds. Rotate people and drink water
- Manual handling of sweepings, bags and equipment
- Falling objects from the upper structure or from deck. Keep people clear below work at height
- Fatigue. Long shifts lead to mistakes

## Frequently asked questions

### Is cargo hold cleaning dangerous?
It can be. The main hazards are enclosed space atmospheres (oxygen deficiency and toxic or flammable gases), high-pressure water, chemicals and working at height. All can be controlled with proper procedures, permits and PPE.

### Can crew enter a hold immediately after washing?
Not without testing the atmosphere first. Washing and chemicals can change the hold atmosphere, so test before entry and after any break.

### What gases can be present in a cargo hold?
Depending on the previous cargo: low oxygen, carbon monoxide, methane, hydrogen sulphide, ammonia, phosphine from fumigation, hydrocarbon vapour and chemical vapour.

### Can crew work alone inside a cargo hold?
No. Enclosed space entry procedures require an attendant at the entrance in contact with those inside, and nobody should work alone.

### What permits are required for cargo hold entry?
Usually an enclosed space entry permit, a permit to work, and where relevant a working-at-height permit and hot work permit, as set out in the ship's safety management system.
