Salt is a strange cargo to clean. It's easy to remove, since it dissolves the moment water touches it, and yet it's one of the most damaging things you can leave behind. Wash a salt hold with seawater, let it dry, and you've just swapped one salt for another.

## Why does salt cause corrosion?

Salt (sodium chloride) does two things that are bad for steel:

- **It's hygroscopic.** Salt pulls moisture out of the air, so a salty surface stays damp long after everything around it has dried.
- **Chloride ions accelerate corrosion.** They break down the protective oxide layer on steel and get under coating at any defect, causing blistering and pitting.

Together, that means salt residue keeps steel wet and makes the wet steel corrode faster. Leave it in a hold and you'll see the result as rust bloom, blistered paint and pitting.

Britannia's guidance notes that lime wash has traditionally been used as a barrier coating for corrosive cargoes like salt, as well as sulphur. Some operators use proprietary barrier coatings instead.

## How should a salt cargo hold be washed?

1. **Remove as much salt as possible during discharge.** Get stevedores to clear ledges and hopper tops.
2. **Sweep out remaining salt.** It may have caked; scrape where needed.
3. **Clean hatch covers and coamings,** including drain channels and rubbers.
4. **Clear bilge wells.** Salt can form hard crusts in wells and strainers.
5. **Initial wash.** Seawater can be used to remove the bulk of the salt, top down. It's salt water removing salt, so it's only a first stage.
6. **Fresh water wash, thoroughly, top down.** This is the stage that removes chlorides. Flush the fixed lines with fresh water first.
7. **Dry quickly and completely.** Ventilate, mop, pump bilges dry.
8. **Test for chlorides** if the next cargo is sensitive.

Britannia advises doing the fresh water wash before the holds dry if possible, since salt residues are easier to remove while still wet.

## How much fresh water is required after salt?

There's no fixed figure. It depends on ship size, the surface area of the holds, how much salt is left and your equipment. As a rough planning approach:

- Estimate the time to rinse each hold thoroughly, top to bottom, with the equipment you'll use
- Multiply by the flow rate. For example, a combi-gun at high volume can use tens of tonnes per hour; an HP unit at 30 l/min uses about 1.8 tonnes per hour
- Add a margin for second rinses in problem areas

For a five-hold Supramax, it's common to plan a fresh water budget measured in tens of tonnes per hold for a thorough rinse with a combi-gun. Many bulk carriers keep fresh water in the aft peak or a dedicated tank for this purpose, and Britannia notes some ships collect air conditioning condensate or take on water while transiting fresh water lakes. Plan it before arrival: running short halfway is a common reason holds fail chloride checks.

## Chloride testing

A simple way to check whether chlorides remain is the silver nitrate test. A few drops of silver nitrate solution in water collected from the hold surface (for example, from a damp wipe or a wash sample) will turn cloudy white if chlorides are present. Test kits are cheap and widely available.

Why does it matter? Britannia points out that if chlorides are later found in moisture in the hold, it can lead to allegations that seawater got into the hold, which complicates the defence of any wet cargo claim. A clean chloride test before loading is evidence for the ship.

## Preparing holds after salt for the next cargo

- **Salt to salt or similar:** normal clean is usually enough. Sweep, wash, fresh water rinse, dry.
- **Salt to grain:** grain clean standard. Thorough fresh water rinse, chloride check, dry, bilge preparation, pre-inspection. See [how to prepare cargo holds for grain](/blog/how-to-prepare-cargo-holds-for-grain).
- **Salt to steel products or other corrosion-sensitive cargo:** chloride removal is critical. Test, and keep records.

## Preventing corrosion after salt

- Barrier coat or limewash before loading salt, where practical
- Wash out with fresh water promptly after discharge
- Dry thoroughly
- Inspect for coating breakdown and corrosion, and repair promptly
- Keep coating in good condition: salt finds every defect

Our guide to [cleaning holds after sulphur](/blog/sulphur-hold-cleaning-and-limewashing) covers limewashing step by step. The same principles apply to salt.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do you clean salt from cargo holds?
Remove as much salt as possible during discharge, sweep, clear bilge wells, wash down, then rinse thoroughly with fresh water from the top down and dry the hold quickly.

### Why does salt cause corrosion in cargo holds?
Salt is hygroscopic, so it keeps steel damp, and chloride ions accelerate corrosion by breaking down the steel's protective layer and getting under coating defects.

### How much fresh water is needed after a salt cargo?
It depends on hold size, equipment and how much salt remains. For a Supramax, plan tens of tonnes per hold for a thorough combi-gun rinse, and make sure the water is on board before cleaning starts.

### How do you test a cargo hold for chlorides?
Use a silver nitrate test on water collected from the hold surfaces. A cloudy white result shows chlorides are present.

### Should salt cargo holds be limewashed?
Lime wash or a barrier coating is a traditional way to protect holds from corrosive cargoes like salt and sulphur. Follow the manufacturer's instructions for application and removal.
