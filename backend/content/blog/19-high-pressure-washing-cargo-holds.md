"Is 200 bar enough?" comes up on almost every job. The honest answer is: for some residues yes, for others not even close, and the pressure on the pump's label is only part of the story. What actually cleans steel is the energy arriving at the surface, and that depends on pressure, flow, nozzle and distance.

## Pressure, flow and distance: what really matters

- **Pressure (bar)** gives the jet its impact force, which is what breaks bonds between residue and steel.
- **Flow (litres per minute)** carries the loosened residue away and determines how much area you can cover.
- **Distance** kills both. Impact force drops off quickly as the jet travels and breaks up. A 500-bar jet at five metres is doing far less than the same jet at 30 cm.
- **Nozzle** shapes the jet: a narrow jet concentrates force on a small spot; a wide fan spreads it.

So a 500-bar unit used from the tank top on a frame 12 metres up will underperform a 250-bar lance held at arm's length. Pressure matters, but access matters more.

## What HP pressure is required for cargo hold cleaning?

| Pressure | Good for | Not good for |
|---|---|---|
| Fire main / combi-gun (around 7–10 bar, high volume) | Rough washing, rinsing, chemical application, reaching height | Stains, films, set residue |
| Around 200 bar | General cleaning of dust and light residue; removing chemical after dwell; rinsing | Old petcoke film, set cement, heavy stains |
| Around 300 bar | Coal staining, most petcoke after chemical treatment, light scale | Hardened cement, heavy scale |
| Around 500 bar | Stubborn petcoke, set mineral deposits, loose rust and scale, cement film | Delicate or weak coatings (risk of damage) |
| 1,000 bar and above (UHP) | Surface preparation, removing coating and heavy scale | Routine hold cleaning |

## Is 200 bar enough?

For clean-to-clean changes and for washing off chemical after it's done its work, 200 bar is usually fine. It struggles with stubborn petcoke, old coal staining and anything that has set. If 200 bar is all you have, compensate with better chemicals, longer dwell, closer access and more time.

## Should I use 300 bar?

300 bar is a good all-round middle. It handles most coal and petcoke jobs after chemical treatment, without much risk to sound coating.

## When is 500 bar required?

500 bar earns its keep on difficult residues: old or green petcoke film, set cement and mineral deposits, heavy staining, and loose rust and scale. It's also faster on large areas of stubborn dirt. We use 500-bar, 30-litre-per-minute units for exactly this kind of work. The trade-off is that at close range, 500 bar can lift weak or poorly adhered paint, so technique matters.

## Can HP washing damage hold coatings?

Yes. High pressure at close range, especially with a pencil (zero-degree) jet, can strip coating that's weak, old or poorly applied. To avoid damage:

- Use a fan or rotating nozzle, not a pencil jet, on painted surfaces
- Keep a sensible stand-off distance, usually 20–50 cm
- Keep the lance moving
- Reduce pressure on weak coating
- Remember: if a 300-bar jet lifts the paint, the paint was probably going to fail inspection anyway

## Can HP washing remove rust?

It removes loose rust and flaking scale well, especially at higher pressure. It won't remove tight, hard-adhering rust, and that usually isn't required for grain clean anyway. See our guide to [removing rust and loose paint](/blog/remove-rust-and-loose-paint-from-cargo-holds).

## What is the best nozzle for hold cleaning?

- **Fan nozzles (15°–40°)** for general cleaning: wider angle for coverage, narrower for impact
- **Rotating (turbo) nozzles:** a spinning pencil jet that gives near-pencil impact over a fan-sized area. Many crews find them dramatically faster on petcoke and coal
- **Pencil (0°) jets:** for spot cleaning stubborn deposits, used carefully

Should you use rotating nozzles? On most stubborn residues, yes. Check that the nozzle is rated for your pump's pressure and flow.

## What HP pump is best for bulk carrier holds?

Look for:

- **Pressure** suited to your typical residues (300–500 bar covers most needs)
- **Flow** high enough to clear the residue (portable units are often around 20–30 l/min)
- **Reliability.** A pump that keeps tripping is worse than a less powerful one that runs
- **Power source** that suits your deck: electric if power is available, diesel if not
- **Serviceability.** Spare seals, valves and nozzles available on board

## How many HP machines are required?

One per team working in a hold, plus a spare. For a five-hold ship with two holds worked at once, that's typically two or three units. For faster jobs, more. Each machine needs its own hoses, lances and operator.

## How many hoses are required?

Enough to reach from the pump to the furthest point in the hold, plus spares. Hold depth plus horizontal run can easily mean 40–60 metres per lance. Carry spare hoses: they're the most common failure.

## How much water is required?

A portable HP unit at 30 l/min uses about 1.8 tonnes of water per hour. Run two units for 10 hours and that's around 36 tonnes, before you count combi-gun washing, which uses far more. Seawater is used for most washing; fresh water is needed for the final rinse. Plan fresh water carefully. Our guide on [hold cleaning time](/blog/how-long-does-hold-cleaning-take) helps estimate hours.

## How can I reduce water consumption?

- Sweep thoroughly before washing
- Use chemicals properly, so residue comes off in one pass
- Use rotating nozzles: more cleaning per litre
- Work close to the surface
- Don't use HP for jobs a broom can do
- Rinse top-down once, not repeatedly

## How do I reach upper frames?

HP washing only works well at close range, so you need access to the upper structure: extension lances for a few metres, then cherry pickers, staging or [rope access](/blog/cleaning-cargo-hold-upper-frames-without-scaffolding).

## Safe use of 500-bar equipment

High-pressure water can cause serious injection injuries that look minor on the skin but need urgent hospital treatment. Rules we work to:

- Only trained operators use HP equipment
- Never point the lance at a person, including yourself
- Wear face shield, waterproof suit, safety boots and gloves
- Use a dead-man trigger, never tie it open
- Inspect hoses and fittings before every use
- Depressurise before disconnecting anything
- Keep others out of the spray zone
- Report any injection injury immediately, however small it looks

## Frequently asked questions

### What HP pressure is required for cargo hold cleaning?
Around 200 bar handles general cleaning and washing off chemicals; 300 bar suits most coal and petcoke after chemical treatment; 500 bar is used for stubborn petcoke, set deposits and loose scale.

### Is 200 bar enough for hold cleaning?
For clean cargo changes and rinsing off chemical, usually yes. For old petcoke film, heavy staining or set residue, it often isn't.

### When is 500 bar required for cargo holds?
For stubborn residue such as old or green petcoke film, set cement, heavy staining and loose rust scale, or to speed up large areas of difficult cleaning.

### Can HP washing damage hold coatings?
Yes, especially with pencil jets at close range on weak coating. Use fan or rotating nozzles, keep a sensible stand-off and keep the lance moving.

### How much water does HP hold cleaning use?
A 30 l/min HP unit uses about 1.8 tonnes per hour. Combi-gun washing uses much more. Plan fresh water separately for the final rinse.
