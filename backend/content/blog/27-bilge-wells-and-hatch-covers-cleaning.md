Two parts of a hold get cleaned last, by the most tired people, and checked first by the surveyor: the bilge wells at the bottom and the hatch covers at the top. Get these two right and you've removed a big share of the reasons holds fail.

## Part 1: Bilge wells

### Why bilge wells matter

The bilge wells collect everything: wash water, fine residue, rust flakes, and whatever else ran down the hold. If they're dirty, they smell, they hold water, and they're where insects live. If the bilge system isn't working, water from the cargo can't be pumped out during the voyage, and you've got a wet cargo claim waiting to happen.

### How should bilge wells be cleaned?

Do it in two stages: once before the main wash, and again at the end.

**Before washing:**

1. Pump out any free water
2. Remove the strainer plates (the bilge well covers)
3. Shovel and bucket out solid residue and sludge
4. Clean the strainer plates and check they're intact
5. Check the bilge suction is clear

Clearing the wells before washing stops the wash from sending a tonne of slurry into a well that's already blocked.

**After washing:**

1. Pump out wash water
2. Remove the sludge that's collected during washing
3. Wash out the wells with fresh water
4. Dry them: mop and sponge until there's no standing water
5. Test the bilge suction and the non-return valves
6. Replace the strainer plates
7. Cover as required for the next cargo

### How do you remove sludge from bilges?

Bilge sludge is a heavy mix of fine residue, water and rust. Scoop it out with shovels and buckets, and haul it up by line or hoist. A portable air-driven pump can handle thinner sludge. Bag it and land it ashore as garbage, or dispose of it under MARPOL rules if the residue isn't HME. For very heavy sludge, specialist [demucking](/services/tank-cleaning/demucking) equipment may be needed.

### Testing bilge suctions

Fill the well with some water and pump it out using the ship's bilge system. Check the suction works, the non-return valve holds, and the bilge alarm (if fitted) works. Many charterers and surveyors ask for this test to be witnessed or recorded.

### Burlap for grain

For grain cargoes, bilge wells are commonly covered with burlap (hessian) over the strainer plate, sealed at the edges with tape or cement wash depending on the requirement. The burlap lets water drain into the well while keeping grain out of the bilge system. Check the shipper's or charterer's exact requirement.

### Isolate the bilge system during cleaning

If you're cleaning one hold while another has cargo in it, isolate the bilge lines between them. Britannia's guidance warns that residues can migrate between holds through the bilge system.

## Part 2: Hatch covers

### Why hatch covers matter

Hatch covers sit directly above the cargo. Anything on their underside (dust, loose paint, rust flakes) drops straight into the hold when the covers open or the ship works. Surveyors know this and look up. And if the covers leak, the cargo gets wet.

### How do you clean hatch covers and their undersides?

**Start with the outside, before washing the hold.**

1. Sweep the tops of the covers
2. Sweep the coaming tops and clear the drain channels
3. Check and clear drain valves
4. Clean the rubber seals and compression bars
5. Clean cross-joints

Britannia recommends sweeping out the coamings and drain channels before washing, and not using compressed air to blow debris out of the channels, since it just moves it around.

**Then the undersides:**

1. Wash the undersides with a combi-gun or HP lance, working from one end to the other
2. Pay attention to girders, stiffeners and the top faces of the panel structure
3. Remove any loose paint or rust with scrapers. This is a common fail point
4. Rinse with fresh water
5. Dry

Cleaning hatch cover undersides is easier with the covers open, so the underside can be reached from deck or from access equipment. Some ships clean the undersides with covers partly open. Follow the ship's procedures for operating hatch covers safely, and never work under a cover that's being moved.

### Rubbers and weathertightness

Old cargo trapped in a hatch rubber stops the seal from closing properly, which causes leaks. Clean the rubbers, check for damage and compression set, and replace damaged sections. Many operators test weathertightness (hose test or ultrasonic test) before a moisture-sensitive cargo like grain.

### What about water running into adjacent holds?

When washing hatch covers or holds with covers partly open, water can run into adjacent holds. Plan the sequence so wash water doesn't contaminate holds that are already clean or loaded.

## Bilge and hatch cover checklist

- [ ] Bilge wells clear of solids before washing
- [ ] Bilge wells cleaned, washed, dried after washing
- [ ] Strainer plates clean and intact
- [ ] Bilge suction and non-return valves tested
- [ ] Bilge wells covered as required
- [ ] Hatch cover tops, coamings and drain channels clean
- [ ] Drain valves clear
- [ ] Rubbers and compression bars clean and intact
- [ ] Hatch cover undersides clean, with no loose paint or rust
- [ ] Weathertightness tested if required

For the full hold checklist, see the [cargo hold inspection checklist](/blog/cargo-hold-inspection-checklist).

## Frequently asked questions

### How should bilge wells be cleaned?
Clear solid residue before washing, then after washing pump out, remove sludge, wash with fresh water, dry, test the suction and non-return valves, and cover as required for the next cargo.

### How do you remove sludge from bilge wells?
Pump out free water, then shovel and bucket out the sludge, using a portable pump for thinner material. Dispose of it ashore or in line with MARPOL rules.

### How do you clean hatch covers and their undersides?
Sweep the tops, coamings and drain channels first, then wash the undersides with a combi-gun or HP lance, remove loose paint and rust, rinse with fresh water and dry.

### Why are bilge wells covered with burlap for grain?
Burlap lets water drain into the bilge well while stopping grain from entering and blocking the bilge system.

### Why do hatch cover undersides fail inspection?
Dust, loose paint and rust on the undersides fall into the cargo when the covers move, so surveyors check them closely.
